SiliconSpyder Build Guide

The Secure WiFi Box

How to build one portable device that provides event WiFi, meters how much internet is used, and watches the airwaves for attacks — all reporting to your live dashboards.

The concept

One box that does three jobs — and sells one story.

Today the Spyder is a passive security monitor. This upgrade turns it into a complete secure event-WiFi appliance: plug it in at a venue and it (1) hands out WiFi to vendors and attendees, (2) tracks how much data the event is using, and (3) keeps watching for rogue access points, evil twins, and other attacks — feeding all of it to your customer and technician dashboards.

The pitch in one line

"Plug in one box and get secure, monitored WiFi for your event — connection, usage, and threat protection, all in a device you can hold in one hand."

The important design principle below: don't build the parts that are already solved. Reliable multi-client WiFi and cellular radios are hard, mature products — buy those. Spend your engineering on the parts that are yours: the integration, the metering, the detection, and the dashboards.

Architecture

How the pieces fit — the signal chain.

Traffic flows left to right. The Pi sits in the middle as the brain: it pulls internet from the cellular modem, hands it to the access point that serves the crowd, and meters everything that passes through. A separate adapter sniffs the air for threats in parallel.

1 · Cellular modem
4G/5G + SIM. The internet source (WAN). Presents as a network interface to the Pi.
→
2 · Raspberry Pi (brain)
Router (NAT + DHCP), usage meter, cloud uplink. Everything flows through here.
→
3 · Access point
Dedicated dual-band AP in bridge mode. Serves WiFi to 25–50 devices.
→
Clients
Vendors & attendees on phones/tablets.
4 · Security adapter (parallel)
USB WiFi adapter in monitor mode running spyder.py — sniffs 802.11 management frames for attacks. Not in the data path.
→
☁ Supabase → Dashboards
The Pi pushes threats and usage to the cloud; your two dashboards read it live.
Privacy = a feature

You meter data volume (how many gigabytes), never data content. The security adapter reads WiFi management frames (network announcements), not people's private traffic. That's a genuine selling point — "we protect the network without snooping on your guests."

The two connectivity parts — picked for you

📶 The access point

Get a WiFi 6, dual-band router and set it to Access Point mode, wired to the Pi. WiFi 6 has a feature (OFDMA) built for talking to many devices at once — exactly your 25–50 case.

ModelWhy~Price
GL.iNet Beryl AX (GL-MT3000) ⭐Tiny, portable, WiFi 6, runs OpenWRT (hackable + reads usage stats). Best match for a portable box. Official $98.99.~$74 (Amazon)
TP-Link Archer AX21 (AX1800)Cheap, WiFi 6, dead-simple AP mode, great value. Often on sale. (You already own this one.)~$48–70

Reality check: one consumer AP comfortably handles ~25–30 active users; a full 50 heavy users pushes a single unit. Start with one, load-test it, add a second if you routinely hit 50.

🛰️ The 4G data plan

First surprise: 25–50 people burn a LOT of data

A 6-hour event, ~40 people, moderate use ≈ ~170 GB per event. Even light use is 40–60 GB. You need a plan measured in hundreds of GB or truly unlimited — not a small phone plan.

What to look for, in priority order:

  1. Coverage at your venue first. In rural Idaho this is everything — the best plan is useless with one bar. Pick the carrier (Verizon / AT&T / T-Mobile) with the strongest signal where you'll deploy; Verizon & AT&T usually win rural. Test on-site if you can.
  2. A real "hotspot / data-only" plan — NOT a phone plan. The #1 trap: most "unlimited" phone plans throttle hotspot/tethering to a tiny cap (10–30 GB). Your box is a hotspot, so you need a plan whose full allowance is usable as hotspot data.
  3. High cap or unlimited with a high throttle threshold. Look at carrier data-only / hotspot device plans, or business/IoT data SIMs. Budget ~$50–90/month.
  4. Avoid cheap MVNOs for this — they often deprioritize your traffic, which collapses under a 50-person load.
This is your biggest recurring cost AND your real capacity ceiling

Lock down the plan and coverage-test the venue before buying hardware — the data plan, not the electronics, is what decides whether 50 users is smooth or unusable.

Physical wiring

How it connects.

Give the Pi three network roles on three different interfaces so nothing conflicts:

wlan1 (USB adapter)Monitor — detectionNothing — it just listens to the air
wlan0 (onboard WiFi)Spare / managementOptional admin access
Signal chain

Modem → (USB) → Pi → (Ethernet, eth0) → Access Point → (WiFi) → Clients. The Pi meters on the WAN interface; the AP just relays.

Enclosure design

Two-tier build — router on top, electronics below.

Three stacked tiers in one enclosed unit with a single flush footprint: the router on top (vented cover, antennas poking out); a vented cooling chamber in the middle with fans that cool both the router above and the electronics below; and the electronics base at the bottom. Because the router is narrower than the module, the voids beside it do double duty — an internal cable chase (router power + Ethernet run straight down to the Pi, no dangling cables) and carry handles. Cellular/GNSS antennas mount on the sides of the base.

The rules that make it work

  • Frame = non-metal only. Plastic / acrylic / 3D-print / wood. Metal blocks WiFi.
  • Vented top cover — don't clamp the vents. A cover is fine (plastic doesn't block WiFi); just give it slots or a gap so the router's heat still escapes upward. The antennas poke through it.
  • Middle = an enclosed vented cooling chamber. Wall it in, put vents on the sides, and mount the fans here — they pull cool air in and blow it across the router's underside and the electronics. Give it a clear in→out path so air actually flows, not just swirls. Cables route through here too.
  • Use the side voids twice. The router is narrower than the module, so the space beside it becomes an internal cable chase (route the router's Ethernet + power straight down to the Pi) — and grip cutouts up there double as carry handles.
  • Cellular/GNSS antennas panel-mount on the base sides via SMA bulkhead + short extension cables.
  • Make the top liftable (off the legs) so the unit flattens for transport, reassembles in 30 seconds.

Build phases

Build it one working layer at a time.

Get each phase fully working before starting the next — that's how you stay sane when something breaks. Test after every phase.

0

Prep the Pi

Goal: a clean, updated, headless Pi.
  • Flash Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit); enable SSH + set your WiFi/hostname in the imager.
  • First boot, then update:
sudo apt update && sudo apt full-upgrade -y
sudo apt install -y nftables dnsmasq vnstat network-manager python3-pip
1

Internet in — the cellular WAN

hardware-specific
Goal: the Pi itself gets online through the cellular modem.
  • Plug in the modem. Detect it and note the interface name:
mmcli -L                 # list modems
nmcli device status      # find the WAN iface (wwan0 / usb0 / ppp0)
  • Bring up the connection with your SIM's APN (get the APN from your carrier):
sudo nmcli connection add type gsm ifname '*' con-name wan apn YOUR_APN
sudo nmcli connection up wan
ping -c3 1.1.1.1         # confirm the Pi is online
Note

Exact steps vary by modem. A QMI/HAT modem may use qmicli instead; a plain USB dongle often "just works" via ModemManager. Confirm the Pi can ping the internet before moving on.

2

Share it — router + client network

Goal: clients on the AP get internet through the Pi.
  • Turn on IP forwarding:
echo 'net.ipv4.ip_forward=1' | sudo tee /etc/sysctl.d/99-router.conf
sudo sysctl -p /etc/sysctl.d/99-router.conf
  • Give the LAN port (eth0) a fixed gateway address:
sudo nmcli connection add type ethernet ifname eth0 con-name lan \
  ipv4.method manual ipv4.addresses 10.10.0.1/24
sudo nmcli connection up lan
  • Hand out addresses with dnsmasq — edit /etc/dnsmasq.conf:
interface=eth0
dhcp-range=10.10.0.50,10.10.0.200,255.255.255.0,12h
dhcp-option=6,1.1.1.1,8.8.8.8
  • NAT so the LAN shares the WAN — /etc/nftables.conf (swap wwan0 for your WAN iface):
table inet nat {
  chain postrouting {
    type nat hook postrouting priority 100;
    oifname "wwan0" masquerade
  }
}
table inet filter {
  chain forward {
    type filter hook forward priority 0; policy drop;
    iifname "eth0" oifname "wwan0" accept
    iifname "wwan0" oifname "eth0" ct state established,related accept
  }
}
sudo systemctl enable --now nftables dnsmasq
  • Set your access point to bridge/AP mode, name the SSID (e.g. Silicon Spyder Secure WiFi), set a WPA2/3 password, and plug it into eth0. Connect a phone → you should have internet.
Milestone

At the end of Phase 2 you have a working mobile hotspot. Everything after this is the "smart" layer.

3

Meter it — usage to the dashboard

Goal: live "data used" numbers flowing to your dashboards.
  • Point vnstat at the WAN interface (real internet usage):
sudo vnstat --add -i wwan0
sudo systemctl enable --now vnstat
vnstat --json d 1        # today's rx/tx in JSON
  • Add a usage table + ingest function to Supabase (same pattern as alerts):
create table if not exists public.usage_metrics (
  id bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  device_id text references public.devices(device_id),
  rx_mb numeric, tx_mb numeric, total_mb numeric,
  created_at timestamptz default now()
);
alter table public.usage_metrics enable row level security;

create or replace function public.ingest_usage(
  p_device_id text, p_device_key text, p_rx numeric, p_tx numeric
) returns void language plpgsql security definer set search_path=public as $$
begin
  if not exists (select 1 from devices d where d.device_id=p_device_id and d.device_key=p_device_key) then
    raise exception 'invalid device credentials';
  end if;
  insert into usage_metrics(device_id,rx_mb,tx_mb,total_mb)
    values (p_device_id,p_rx,p_tx,p_rx+p_tx);
  update devices set last_seen=now() where device_id=p_device_id;
end; $$;
grant execute on function public.ingest_usage(text,text,numeric,numeric) to anon;
  • A tiny poller on the Pi reads vnstat and pushes every minute (usage_push.py):
import json, subprocess, time, urllib.request
URL="https://micnxjnhxdxlqftzmazx.supabase.co/rest/v1/rpc/ingest_usage"
KEY="sb_publishable_..."   # your anon key
DEV,DK="spyder-01","PASTE_YOUR_REAL_DEVICE_KEY_HERE"   # never commit the real key to git
while True:
    d=json.loads(subprocess.check_output(["vnstat","--json","d","1"]))
    day=d["interfaces"][0]["traffic"]["day"][-1]
    rx=day["rx"]/1048576; tx=day["tx"]/1048576   # bytes -> MB
    body=json.dumps({"p_device_id":DEV,"p_device_key":DK,"p_rx":rx,"p_tx":tx}).encode()
    req=urllib.request.Request(URL,body,{"apikey":KEY,"Authorization":"Bearer "+KEY,"Content-Type":"application/json"})
    try: urllib.request.urlopen(req,timeout=10)
    except Exception as e: print("push failed",e)
    time.sleep(60)

Then we add a "Data used today" tile to both dashboards — that part I can wire for you.

4

Protect it — run detection in parallel

Goal: the Spyder engine you already built runs alongside, untouched.
  • Put the USB adapter (wlan1) in monitor mode and run spyder.py as a service (you've done this).
  • Because the box now is the trusted network, treat your own SSID as known-good in the baseline so it never self-alerts.
  • Both services run at once: detection on wlan1, routing/metering on eth0/wwan0 — they don't interfere.
5

Make it portable & resilient

optional / advanced
Goal: runs on battery, survives the real world.
  • Power: size the battery for Pi + modem + AP together (measure the draw, add margin).
  • Heat: active cooling — throttling under load causes mystery slowdowns.
  • Fail-open (advanced): a bypass so if the Pi dies, the modem still reaches the AP and the event keeps internet. This removes the single-point-of-failure risk for a real product.
  • Auto-start everything on boot via systemd; test a cold power-on with no keyboard/screen.

Risks & gotchas

Know these before you build.

Single point of failure

The Pi is now in the traffic path — if it crashes, the event loses internet. Fine for a demo; for a paid product, add the Phase-5 fail-open bypass.

Data plan & signal = the real ceiling

50 people streaming needs serious bandwidth and a strong cellular signal. This, not the electronics, is what usually makes or breaks an event. Test at (or near) the real venue.

Heat & power

Routing + AP + detection together draw real power and generate heat. Undersized battery or no cooling = random failures. Measure both.

WiFi capacity is a real engineering limit

25–50 clients is genuinely demanding. Use proper AP hardware (this is why we don't use the Pi's own radio), plan channels, and load-test before you promise a number to a customer.

Be a good network citizen

You provide connectivity and meter volume only — don't inspect guests' traffic content. Consider a simple terms/acceptable-use notice. Keeps you clean legally and reputationally.

Testing checklist

Prove each layer before the event.

  • ☐ Pi reaches the internet over cellular (ping passes).
  • ☐ A phone on the AP gets an address and loads pages.
  • ☐ Load test: connect as many devices as you can, stream video on several at once, watch for drops.
  • ☐ Usage numbers appear on the dashboard and climb as you use data.
  • ☐ Trigger a test attack (evil-twin) → it appears on both dashboards + alerts.
  • ☐ Cold boot with no screen/keyboard → everything auto-starts.
  • ☐ Run on battery for the full expected event duration.
  • ☐ Range check: walk to the edges of the venue footprint.
